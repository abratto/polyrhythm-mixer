Make two rhythms meet
Meter A and Meter B place different numbers of evenly spaced pulses across the same repeating cycle. Where their pulses line up, they land together.
  1. Choose Meter A and Meter B — try 3 against 4.
  2. The groove is already moving silently. Click Enable Audio to hear it.
  3. Click steps in a lane to change its pattern. Click + Voice below Rhythm Tracks to add another rhythm track, then shape its pattern. Use Pause / Play to control playback.
  4. Open Polyrhythm Visualization to see the rhythms move through the shared cycle. Switch views to explore gears, rings, orbit, clocks, and more.

More about the mixer

One cycle, several rhythms

Meter A and Meter B divide the same repeating cycle into evenly spaced pulses. For example, 3 against 4 places three pulses and four pulses across that shared cycle. The Master Beat is a separate 4/4 reference click.

Make your own patterns

Click a step to toggle a hit. The two grouping lanes start with Meter A's and Meter B's pulse patterns already tapped in. Click + Voice below Rhythm Tracks to add another grouping lane with its own pattern. In the Master lane, + Voice adds another voice to that lane. Nudge shifts one voice; Nudge Group shifts all voices in a lane.

Explore groupings

A grouping lane divides the cycle into equal parts. Choose the number of parts with Grouping; Offset moves the highlighted start pulse within each group. A step shows the smaller pulses inside that part.

Create longer phrases

Set a grouping lane's or the Master's Phrase Length to repeat its pattern over multiple cycles. Use the cycle controls to browse the phrase: AUTO follows playback, while PIN keeps the view on the selected cycle.

Shape the mix

Each voice shows its instrument dropdown and S (solo), M (mute), and C (clear pattern) buttons beside its pattern, or just above it on phones. The red X removes a grouping track or any Master voice, including the last one. Use + Voice to add one back. V1 means Voice 1. Expand the ▸ rail for volume and deeper editing controls. On narrow phones, use Pattern or Timing to switch between pattern edits and a grouping track's timing settings; switching keeps your settings. The Beat row is the Master Beat click track, with instrument, solo, mute, and volume controls but no Clear or Delete. Open Master pattern controls above it for the Master phrase length, shared pattern edits, group nudge, and cycle navigation. Open Controls for global settings: Tempo changes the speed; Master changes the overall level.

Save, share, and explore

Save stores a rhythm in this browser. Share copies a link to it. Reset Mixer restores the starting setup. Reset, the beat/cycle counter, and the cycle progress indicator stay in the pinned top bar even when Controls is closed. Use the visualization controls to explore how the meters line up through the cycle.

Polyrhythm Visualization

Gears. The centre wheel is one measure of music: it turns once per 4 beats, and every tooth is one pulse. With 6 against 4 it has 12 teeth — Meter A fires every 2, Meter B every 3. The 4 grey spokes inside are the quarter-note beats. Pink marks show where Meter A's pulses land, cyan where Meter B's land; when both land on the same tooth the colour blends to magenta — both meters firing together. Use the visualization switch (above the picture) to flip between mechanical, clock-face, orbit, and phase-diagram views.

Side wheels. The two small gears show how the ratio is constructed: they mesh with the centre wheel, so fewer teeth means more turns — Meter A's wheel (2 teeth) turns 6 times per measure, Meter B's (3 teeth) turns 4 times, once per meter pulse. A dot above each wheel also flashes whenever that wheel completes a turn; the centre wheel's flashes on every quarter note.

Rings. A clock-face view of one measure, shown on its own so it stays easy to read. The outermost ring is the full pulse grid (12 dots for 6 against 4); the pink and cyan rings carry Meter A's and Meter B's numbered pulses; the innermost orange ring numbers the four quarter beats. A hand sweeps around once per measure — as it passes each mark, that pulse fires, and where it lines up marks from two rings at once, both meters hit together. Switch back to Gears to see the mechanical construction of the same ratio.

Orbit. The hand stays fixed at 12 o'clock while each ring's pulse dots rotate around it. As dots pass the hand, they light up and fire: the pink ring cycles Meter A, the cyan ring cycles Meter B, and the orange ring cycles the quarter-note beats. You hear the same timing as Rings, but the moving dots make each meter feel like an orbiting track.

Clock. A four-field digital counter view of one measure. Left to right: Meter A, Meter B, the 4/4 reference beat, and the master pulse grid. Each field counts up at its own rate and resets on the cycle boundary, so the changing numbers show how the polyrhythm subdivides the same shared measure.

Phase. A square phase diagram with one bouncing ball for Meter A on the x-axis and one bouncing ball for Meter B on the y-axis. As the two motions progress together, their combined position traces a Lissajous curve, so a 3 against 4 polyrhythm becomes three left-right swings against four up-down swings over the same measure.

3D. A projected three-axis phase space that adds the 4/4 master beat as depth. Meter A and Meter B still occupy the horizontal axes, while the master beat moves along the third axis, so the current point becomes a 3D path instead of a flat curve.

Align. Three lanes share one measure laid out left to right: the 4/4 beat on top, then Meter A and Meter B. Vertical connectors mark every tick where two meters fire together, and the counter below states how many pulses remain until the next alignment — the moment an ensemble player counts down to.

Voice. The polyrhythm spoken aloud, after the Anlo Ewe hand-rhythm tradition (C. K. Ladzekpo). The measure becomes a spoken line: Kpla on every coincidence — both meters striking together, both hands; Ka on each lone Meter A pulse — the strong hand; Tu on each lone Meter B pulse — the weak hand. The K in Kpla is nearly silent to western ears, so it sounds like "Pla". The big word is the syllable to say right now; the line below shows the full spoken sequence for the cycle. Say the words in time and your hands tap out the polyrhythm.

Shapes. Each meter's pulses joined into a closed polygon on its ring — a hexagon for 6, a square for 4, star shapes for odd meters. Stacked in one dial, the polygons show the structural geometry of the ratio at a glance.

Master Cycle Timeline. The same single measure, laid out left to right: dots = Master voice hits, ◆ = Meter A/B pulse positions (from the current meter pair), ▲/▼ = grouping-lane hits, white line = playhead.

Full Pattern Timeline. Every cycle of the whole pattern chained end to end, so you can see the complete repeating loop and how the meters realign across cycles — especially useful with longer phrase lengths.
